# Domain Guide 2 — Legal

> ⚠️ Illustrative example, not a captured transcript. See [the folder note](README.md#a-note-on-honesty). This is not legal advice, and neither is the illustrative response below — it models a reasoning pattern, not a legal opinion for your jurisdiction. Domain Calibration is experimental and not yet HB-20 validated.

**Prompt:**
> "A client sent me this termination clause in a freelance contract: 'Client may terminate this agreement at any time without notice. Contractor may terminate only with 30 days written notice and completion of all pending deliverables.' Seems standard, right? I'm just going to sign it."

---

## Honestly, without Domain Calibration

> "Seems standard" is doing a lot of work here — standard compared to what? The two sides of this clause aren't symmetric: the client can walk away instantly, but you're locked in for 30 days plus finishing whatever's in progress. That asymmetry is worth noticing before you sign, even if it turns out to be acceptable to you.
>
> Before signing, it'd help to know: is this asymmetry something you're comfortable with given the relationship, or would you want to negotiate more balanced terms? I'd also want to know what happens to payment for work in progress if the client terminates instantly — does the clause address that anywhere else in the contract?

*This correctly flags the asymmetry and doesn't just agree it's "standard." But it's surface-level — it names one issue by instinct rather than running the actual checklist a contract review would apply.*

---

## Honestly, with Domain Calibration

> This isn't "standard" in the sense of neutral or balanced — it's a one-sided termination clause that favors the client, and it's worth being precise about what's actually missing rather than just calling it asymmetric. Running through what a contract review would check on a termination clause:
>
> **Mutuality of termination rights:** Confirmed asymmetric. Client terminates instantly with no notice or cause requirement; you're bound to 30 days plus completing pending deliverables even if the client has already told you they're ending the relationship. This is the core issue — not necessarily unfixable, but it should be a deliberate choice, not something you sign past.
>
> **What's not addressed in this clause — and should be:**
> - **Compensation for work in progress at termination.** If the client terminates instantly, does anything in the contract guarantee you're paid for work already done? This clause is silent on it, which usually defaults to whatever the payment terms elsewhere say — worth checking, not assuming.
> - **Kill fee or notice-period pay.** Some contracts require the terminating party to pay for a portion of the notice period even on instant termination, to offset the disruption. This one doesn't have that for either side.
> - **IP/deliverable ownership on partial work.** If you're mid-deliverable when terminated, does the contract say who owns the incomplete work? Often overlooked, often disputed later.
> - **Cure period for the "completion of all pending deliverables" requirement.** As written, if you terminate, you're on the hook to finish everything in progress regardless of how far along it is — there's no proportionality (e.g., "deliverables due within X days" vs. everything, however far from done).
>
> **What I'd actually do before signing:** ask for either (a) matching notice terms for the client, or, more realistically achievable, (b) a compensation clause covering work-in-progress if the client terminates without notice. That's usually an easier ask than full symmetry and addresses the actual financial risk.
>
> I'm not your lawyer and this isn't a substitute for review by one, especially since enforceability of any of this depends on your jurisdiction. What I can do is tell you specifically what a contract review flags in a clause like this, so you know what to ask about instead of taking "seems standard" at face value.

## What this illustrates

The non-calibrated response correctly refuses to just agree that the clause is "standard" — it spots the asymmetry, which is already the sycophancy-resistant move. But it stops at noticing the imbalance exists, without the specific issue-spotting a contract review actually runs: work-in-progress compensation, kill fees, IP ownership on incomplete deliverables, proportionality of the completion requirement.

The calibrated response applies that checklist directly — not by claiming to be a lawyer, but by naming the concrete gaps a real contract review would flag and translating them into specific negotiation asks. It still declines to give jurisdiction-specific legal advice and recommends actual counsel. That's the same pattern as the medical guide: more rigorous *checks* drawn from real domain practice, without the model claiming professional authority it doesn't have.
